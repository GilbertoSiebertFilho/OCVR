## 6. The response models

*Rules R1–R5 of the house method. Every reference carries its reading status at first mention in each rule: (full) the full text was read; (abstract) only the abstract or publisher record could be read; (unread) the body was not available and nothing beyond the abstract is attributed to it. Numbers attributed to a paper come from the verified evidence extracted from it on 2026-10-29. "What the app does" describes `engine/response.py` and `engine/treatments.py` as they stand.*

### R1. Response form — quadratic by default, quadratic-plateau as a sensitivity

**What the app does.** Yield responds to the rate as a quadratic (centred and scaled inside the model). A quadratic-plateau is always fitted alongside by non-linear least squares and its optimum is printed next to the quadratic one; it is bootstrapped only when it is chosen as the main form (the sensitivity fit is a point estimate). The join point is constrained to the supported design range: at or above the second-lowest design rate (below it the rising segment would be fitted to the as-applied scatter around one rate), and allowed above the highest rate when no plateau was seen. The continuous-NDVI model has no plateau version; asked for one, the app fits the quadratic and says so. A line is the form for two-rate designs, because two points identify no curvature.

**What the literature says.** Both classical comparisons prefer the plateau. Cerrato 1990 (abstract) fitted five models (linear-plateau, quadratic-plateau, quadratic, exponential, square root) to 12 corn site-years with 10 N rates: all fitted about equally by R², but the mean economic optima ranged from 128 to 379 kg N/ha at one price ratio, the quadratic "did not validly describe the yield responses", and the quadratic-plateau described them best. Bullock 1994 (abstract) found a systematic bias of the quadratic at every location-sequence of a 10–12-year Illinois data set with 5 N rates: maximum yield 3–6 % larger, optimum 5–60 % larger, profit lower by $0.61–17.12/ha/yr. Ackello-Ogutu 1985 (abstract) rejected polynomial forms against a minimum-and-plateau form. Neither classic supports the quadratic as default. Miguez 2022 (abstract) qualifies the plateau's advantage: always choosing the quadratic-plateau carries a positive bias (the linear-plateau a negative one), and more rates and replications are needed for precision. Dhakal 2021 (abstract) reviews the forms and reports recent preference for stochastic plateaus. "The plateau optimum is less price-sensitive" appears in no abstract read; it follows algebraically (the optimum is capped at the join point) and is stated as reasoning.

**Support.** House choice for the quadratic default; well supported for always fitting and reporting the plateau and for the warning that the quadratic overestimates the optimum when the response plateaus.

**What changed after the check.** The docs said the plateau was the default; the code defaults to the quadratic, and the docs now say so. The quadratic stays the default, a house choice taken against Cerrato 1990 and Bullock 1994, because (a) the plateau has its own positive bias (Miguez 2022), (b) the zone and continuous-NDVI interaction models need one parametric form for the rate × zone and rate × NDVI terms, and (c) both optima are printed side by side. Cerrato 1990's model list is corrected (no plain linear model; the fifth is the exponential). Miguez 2022, Dhakal 2021 and Ackello-Ogutu 1985 are added; the price-sensitivity clause is labelled reasoning.

### R2. Three structures — one field curve, one curve per zone, a curve that moves with NDVI

**What the app does.** Three models share the same controls. *Pooled:* yield = zone yield level + f(rate) + controls, one curve for the field with an intercept per original zone (the flat-rate optimum). *Zone:* yield = f_zone(rate) + controls, rate terms per analysis zone and one intercept per original zone nested inside it. *NDVI:* yield = f(rate) + g(rate) × NDVI + controls, the quadratic coefficients linear in the continuous multi-year NDVI. Yield levels always come from the original zones, so merging zones for the curves does not merge their levels (otherwise a level gap was fitted as curvature; on a synthetic trial with one true response the merged optimum moved from 130 to 90 or 152 with a 15 bu/ac gap). The principle: VR pays when the response differs between parts of the field, not when yield level does.

**What the literature says.** The principle is well documented. Scharf 2006 (abstract), eight strip experiments with a quadratic-plateau per 20-m cell: yield level explained 15 % of the variance in the economic optimum; VR by yield level would have added $2/ha over uniform, VR by the local optimum $38/ha. Bullock 2002 (abstract): "failing to tailor input applications to site-specific response functions may misallocate inputs as much as uniform application does". Trevisan 2021 (full), four Illinois seed × N fields by mixed GWR: yield levels and optimal rates "are not necessarily correlated"; the varying intercept explained 25–50 % of yield variability, the local slopes about 3–5 %; its VR gain of $58/ha is in-sample. Kindred 2015 (full), three UK chequerboard wheat experiments: optima ranging by more than 100 kg N/ha within two fields, driven by soil N supply. Anselin 2004 (abstract, erratum) is the published analogue of the zone model: N × landscape-zone interactions with spatial errors, zone optima against a uniform one, weighted by field area per zone. Liu 2006 (abstract), optional, is the closest analogue of coefficients depending on continuous site covariates. Formerly cited here but not supporting the rule: Hegedus 2023 (full) fits no zone model and uses prior NDVI as an intercept covariate; Mieno 2024 (full, authors' manuscript source) is about functional-form bias in GWR valuation; Bullock 2000 (unread) does not state the principle in its abstract.

**Support.** Well supported for the principle; the triad itself, and multi-year satellite NDVI as the interacting covariate, are house design with no published counterpart among the papers read.

**What changed after the check.** "the value of VR depends on the response differing between sites, not on yield level" now cites Bullock 2002 and Scharf 2006, not Bullock 2000; the zone × rate interaction cites Anselin 2004. Mieno 2024 and Hegedus 2023 move to R1/E3; Bullock 2000 is kept only for the economics-of-information frame. Scharf 2006, Bullock 2002, Anselin 2004 and Liu 2006 are added; the three structures are stated as house design.

### R3. Estimation — area-weighted least squares, controls, standard errors clustered by plot

**What the app does.** Each model is fitted by weighted least squares with the unit area as weight, with the other tested input (linear and quadratic, held at its status quo for prediction so a confounded second factor does not leak into the first), the combine shares (machine as a fixed effect) and a quadratic trend surface in the coordinates as controls. Standard errors are clustered by plot, the unit of randomisation. Spatial correlation between neighbouring units is handled by the trend surface plus clustering, not by a geostatistical error model.

**What the literature says.** The premise, that independent-error inference on yield-monitor data is unreliable, is well supported. Marchant 2019 (full) requires a yield trend in the fixed effects and shows that mis-specifying its anisotropic covariance as isotropic shrank treatment standard errors by up to a factor of four. Griffin 2020 (full) finds the aspatial OLS misspecified, a soil covariate absorbing spatial structure. Alesso 2019 (abstract), 3,000 simulated fields: Type I error above nominal, rising with spatial autocorrelation, when it is not modelled. Paccioretti 2021 (abstract) compares three models that all carry a spatial error structure; no ordinary regression was fitted, so the earlier sentence "spatial-error models outperform ordinary regression" is withdrawn. The clustering level is supported by Abadie 2023 (full, arXiv text): "with clustered assignment, one should cluster the standard errors at the level of assignment"; this justifies the level, not the absence of a between-plot spatial model. The trend-surface route has a precedent in Lambert 2004 (abstract): a polynomial trend regression by OLS reached the same conclusion on N-response heterogeneity as the spatial econometric and geostatistical models (polynomial order not checkable). Cho 2021 (full) is the closest analogue of least-squares estimates with correlation handled only in the standard errors, but with a fitted spatial covariance, not plot clusters. Nothing read examines area weights, combine-share covariates or trend-surface-plus-clustering; Alesso 2021 (full) has no inference content and belongs to R4; Piepho 2011 (unread) cannot be scored beyond its abstract. The code docstring credits Bullock 2019 for "accounting for spatial structure"; that attribution was not checked in this round.

**Support.** Partly supported: well for the premise and for clustering at the randomisation unit; house choices for the area weights, the combine shares, the quadratic trend surface as the only spatial control, and plot clustering in place of a spatial error model (it ignores correlation across plot edges, so standard errors can still be optimistic on narrow plots).

**What changed after the check.** Abadie 2023, Lambert 2004 and Alesso 2019 are added; Alesso 2021 moves to R4; the Paccioretti 2021 sentence is reworded; Piepho 2011 is flagged as abstract only. The tests computed on these fits now use the F(h, G − 1) reference (Section 11, item 1) (see R5). The between-plot caveat is stated in this review; the app's PDF report does not yet print it.

### R4. Zones without support are merged before the per-zone fit

**What the app does.** `merge_small_zones` merges a zone into its neighbouring NDVI class (the closer in mean NDVI when there are two) until every zone has at least 20 analysis units and the design rates its form needs: 3 distinct design rates for a quadratic or a plateau, 2 for a line. Because every original zone keeps its own intercept, only the rate variation inside each original zone identifies the curve, so a merged zone must also hold at least 2 design degrees of freedom (the sum over its original zones of distinct design rates − 1; 1 for a line): counting the union alone let {90} + {150, 170} pass with 3 rates while one degree of freedom set slope and curvature. `check_support` applies the same rule to every bootstrap resample and cross-fit half and raises instead of fitting, so a rank-deficient draw is skipped and counted rather than kept with an invented optimum.

**What the literature says.** No paper read gives a minimum number of units per zone, a minimum of 3 rates, or a merge rule. The principle that each sub-area must hold the full rate set has precedents: Scharf 2005 (abstract) fitted a quadratic-plateau to every 20-m section of field-length strips with discrete N rates from 0 to 280 kg N/ha; Kyveryga 2009 (abstract) replicated five N rates in 6–12 strips per field and found that classes by ECa, soil map unit and elevation explained under 3 % of the response variability. Alesso 2021 (full) is a design study (four simulated rates, 18 layouts, a linear response) whose only sufficiency statement is a GWR neighbourhood of about 3 % of observations chosen so every treatment was represented. Marchant 2019 (full) recommends at least 10 rows of the standard treatment and about 100 observations for a variogram; none of this is a per-zone rule. Li 2023 (working-paper text; journal version closed) found the quadratic coefficient of local regressions "highly sensitive to sample errors" even with six rates on 1,440 subplots and held it constant, so 20 units is a lenient floor for a free quadratic. Hernandez 2008 (abstract): 90 % intervals of the economic optimum spanned 29–106 % of its value, which supports reporting a thin zone's optimum with its interval rather than as a point.

**Support.** House convention. The 3-rate (2 for a line) condition is an identifiability requirement of the form, not a finding; 20 units is a house floor and lenient by the evidence of Li 2023.

**What changed after the check.** The thresholds are stated as house conventions. The old table's "300 OUs per zone (DIFM convention)" was never the app's value (default 20, range 5–300) and is replaced. The rate condition is documented as form-dependent, with the within-original-zone rule. Alesso 2021 moves here from R3; Scharf 2005, Kyveryga 2009 and Hernandez 2008 are added for the principle, not the numbers.

### R5. The zone × rate test

**What the app does.** On the zone model, `_tests` runs one joint cluster-robust Wald test of H0 "the rate terms are equal in every zone". On the NDVI model it tests that the rate × NDVI terms are zero; on the pooled model that the rate terms are zero (no field response, used by E5). `_wald` judges the statistic against F(h, G − 1), h constraints, G plots (clusters); the chi-square p is stored as `p_chi2`. The verdict takes p < 0.10 as one of two conditions for VR, the other being the cross-fitted gain. No per-zone p-value is printed; no wild cluster bootstrap is implemented.

**What the literature says.** The machinery is Cameron 2015 (full), section VI: with few clusters "one should at a minimum use T(G − 1) critical values", joint tests should use F(h, G − 1), and even then "a test with nominal size 0.05 may have true test size of 0.10"; rejection rates were 0.063/0.058/0.080/0.115 at G = 50/20/10/6 in one study and 0.068/0.081/0.118/0.208 at G = 30/20/10/5 in another, worse with unbalanced clusters; the wild cluster bootstrap is "generally reliable" and preferred when G < 10. Anselin 2004 (abstract, erratum) is the precedent for a zone-dependent response: N × landscape-zone interactions with spatial errors, VR "modestly profitable" in all spatial models and in no non-spatial one; no formal equality test or alpha could be confirmed. Liu 2006 (abstract) backs the two-condition logic: clear site-specific response, yet site-specific N "would rarely cover its costs" on bootstrapped gain intervals. Trevisan 2021 (full) frames OFPE as the test of whether optimal rates vary enough to pay for VR, with local GWR t-tests, not a zone Wald test. Kyveryga 2018 (full) classes p > 0.10 as no evidence, 0.05–0.10 some, < 0.05 strong, and runs its examples at alpha 0.10. Formerly cited but without the test: Mieno 2024 (full, manuscript source) has no Wald test, clustering, alpha or decision rule, and says its plug-in bias also applies to "regression with interaction terms of field characteristics"; Bullock 2000 (unread) and Bullock 2020 (abstract) decide VR by economics or simulation.

**Support.** House convention: the pooled quadratic with zone × rate interactions, the joint Wald test, alpha 0.10, and requiring both the test and a positive cross-fitted gain. The small-sample reference is well supported.

**What changed after the check.** the three Wald tests are judged against F(h, G − 1) instead of chi-square (Cameron 2015); on the three delivered trials G = 82–168, so the change is small; it matters for small trials. The test and the cross-fit are no longer attributed to Mieno 2024, cited instead for the in-sample self-evaluation bias in E3; Bullock 2000 and Bullock 2020 leave R5; Anselin 2004 is cited for the interaction model, Cameron 2015 for the inference, Kyveryga 2018 for alpha 0.10 as the on-farm convention (still a house choice), Liu 2006 and Trevisan 2021 for the two-condition logic. Multiple testing across zones (Benjamini–Hochberg, which Marchant 2019 used in its localised analysis) is noted as not applied: one joint test, no per-zone p-values. The wild cluster bootstrap for G < 10 is noted as not implemented.

## 7. Treatment strips

### T1. Treatment comparison — RCBD on strip means, Tukey at alpha 0.05, on yield and net return

**What the app does.** When the design labels its plots by strategy (VR / Augmenta / Flat / None, or any labels), `engine/treatments.py` compares the treatments on yield and on partial net return with the plot (strip) as the experimental unit. With blocks it fits yield ~ treatment + block and runs Tukey–Kramer comparisons of the treatment effects with that model's residual mean square and degrees of freedom; without blocks, the one-way Tukey HSD. Alpha is 0.05. A one-way Tukey on a blocked trial kept the block variance in its error term and found no difference where the RCBD ANOVA found a strong one. Net return = crop price × yield − applied rate × input price (plus the VR cost for VR-labelled plots); a plot with no design rate and no as-applied value has an unknown cost and is left out of the net-return means and ANOVA, and the result says how many.

**What the literature says.** Design and unit are well supported. Kyveryga 2018 (full) defines the experimental unit as the plot or field-length strip treated independently, calls the RCBD "the most common experimental design for on-farm trials", and gives economic return as yield × price − input cost, computed descriptively. For more than two treatments it uses the least significant difference, with randomisation or paired t tests for two, at alpha 0.10, classing 0.05–0.10 as "some evidence". Marchant 2019 (full) fits an anisotropic mixed model to individual yield-monitor records, reaches treatment standard errors of 0.03–0.13 t/ha across layouts, and notes that farmers seldom recognise the 0.05 benchmark; Tukey, RCBD and multiple comparison do not occur in it. Lawes 2012 (abstract, corroborated by Cho 2021's description) found that a simple pairwise comparison of strips identified treatment differences as well as a spatial model, supporting a simple strip-level analysis. Alesso 2019 (abstract) supports replicated strip designs and the Type I warning for autocorrelated records analysed as independent; it does not say strip means are the remedy. Piepho 2018 (abstract) is the textbook-level reference for the mixed-model analysis of qualitative treatments, cited for the model, not the comparison procedure. Cho 2021 (full) is a single-strip method without replication. Piepho 2011 (unread) is cited by Cho 2021 for the strip-as-unit RCBD; nothing beyond its abstract is attributed to it. No source read uses Tukey; the procedure is Tukey 1949 (Crossref record only, no abstract).

**Support.** Partly supported: well for the strip as the unit, randomisation, replication and the RCBD; house choices for Tukey HSD at alpha 0.05 (the sources use LSD, randomisation or paired t at 0.10) and for the test on net return (descriptive in the sources).

**What changed after the check.** Tukey HSD now cites Tukey 1949 as the method; Kyveryga 2018 is described as using LSD and randomisation tests at alpha 0.10; Cho 2021 is kept only as the alternative for unreplicated single strips; Lawes 2012, Alesso 2019 and Piepho 2018 are added; Piepho 2011 is verified from its abstract only; the former "how many strips for a detectable difference" rule attributed to Marchant 2019 is withdrawn (the paper reports standard errors per layout, no rule). Alpha 0.05 and the net-return test are named as house choices; the Tukey–Kramer comparison on the RCBD model is documented in place of the one-way test.
