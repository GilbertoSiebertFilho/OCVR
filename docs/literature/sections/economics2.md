## 8. Economics and the decision (continued)

### E7. Price sensitivity: the crop price × input price grid

**What the app does.** After the verdict, `economics.price_grid` re-runs the decision (`analyse`, 81-point rate grid) at every cell of a 7 × 7 grid of crop price × input price. Both axes span −30 % to +30 % of the prices entered in step 3 (`Prices.input_price_range_pct`, applied to both prices in `project.py`), so the steps are 10 %. Each cell records the best flat rate, the zone optima, the VR gain over the best flat rate and the gain over the status quo; the PDF prints them as a pivot. The grid runs on the response form actually fitted (quadratic by default, quadratic-plateau as the sensitivity, R1); nothing is assumed about how that form reacts to prices.

**What the literature says.** Pannell 2006 (abstract, plus the author's own full summary of the paper; the publisher's text returned 403) is the reason a price-driven shift of the optimum matters little: payoff functions are flat near the optimum, so even large departures from the optimal decision change the payoff very little; in his lime example the optimum is 2.7 t/ha and any rate from 1.8 to 4.3 t/ha pays within 5 % of the maximum. Nothing in the abstract or the summary mentions prices, price ratios or a grid. Pannell 2019 (abstract only) links flatness to VR: a flatter payoff reduces the benefit of adjusting rates within a field, and the standard deviation of site-specific payoff slopes at the uniform optimum tracks that benefit; that prices scale those slopes is our inference, not the paper's statement. Kitchen 2010 (abstract only) is the one empirical case of a VR gain that moves with prices: in 16 corn N experiments (0–235 kg N/ha) the optimum varied by more than 100 kg N/ha within 13 of 16 fields, sensing earned about $25–50/ha, and its value rose as fertiliser cost rose relative to grain price; the abstract gives neither price levels nor a range. Bachmaier 2012 (full text) gives the mathematics by form: in the quadratic the optimum is where the slope equals r/p, so it and its confidence set move with prices; in the linear-plus-plateau the optimum sits at the transition and does not depend on prices unless r/p exceeds the rising slope. The paper uses one fixed ratio (r/p = 0.0054545: 1.20 EUR/kg N, 220 EUR/Mg grain) and runs no price sensitivity. Bullock 1994 (abstract only) bears on E7 only indirectly: the quadratic predicts an optimum 5–60 % above the quadratic-plus-plateau and reduces profit by $0.61–17.12/ha/yr, so the grid must be run on the form fitted. Rajsic 2008 (abstract only) says nothing about prices and does not support E7.

**Support.** Partly supported. The reason for the grid (flat payoff; a gain that depends on the price ratio) is published; the ±30 % span, the 10 % steps and the two-dimensional crop × input grid in place of a single price ratio are house choices (the 2-D grid gives the same optimum as the ratio but a different $/ac gain).

**What changed after the check.** The sentence "a plateau optimum sits at the join point, so it is price-insensitive" is true only for the linear-plateau (Bachmaier 2012, section 2.2); the quadratic-plateau the app fits has its optimum below the join point, where the slope of the quadratic segment equals r/p, so it still moves with prices, bounded above by the join. The sentence was corrected. Rajsic 2008 was moved out of E7 (it belongs to E5 and E11); Kitchen 2010, Bachmaier 2012 and Pannell 2019 were added; the ±30 % grid is named here as a house choice.

### E8. The realised trial and the ex-post cost of the trial to the grower

**What the app does.** `economics.realised` takes the analysed units (plot × zone) and computes, for each, the net return actually obtained at the applied rate and what the same unit would have returned at the status quo, at the best flat rate, at the recommended flat rate and at its zone's VR rate. The counterfactual keeps the unit's own residual, y_cf = y_obs + ŷ(r_cf) − ŷ(r_applied): the model only moves the unit along its zone curve. The VR return is net of the VR cost; a status quo outside the trial range gets the bounded extension of E9, and one too far outside is not priced (`sq_too_far`). Area-weighted sums give the field differences (trial − status quo, VR − status quo, VR − flat) and a table by zone. With two inputs `project.realised_joint` computes the counterfactual once with every input at its status quo at once: the fitted yield is additive in the controls, so y_cf = y_obs + Σ_i (y_sq,i − y_obs); inputs without a priced status quo are named and left out. The PDF quotes the result once: the trial rates cost X $/ac against the status quo, × plot acres (extrapolated from the analysed acres) = total, "the ex-post cost of the trial to the grower, the quantity on-farm experimentation programmes compensate (DIFM pays a fixed amount per acre; Puntel 2024), computed here from the realised units"; within 0.5 $/ac the trial cost nothing, and a trial that out-earned the status quo leaves nothing to compensate.

**What the literature says.** Bullock 2019 (full text, re-read passage by passage) supplies the inputs only: cooperating farmers provide the "typical" way the field would be managed without a DIFM experiment (baseline seeding and fertiliser rates), which is the grower-declared baseline; plot interiors are cut into subplots of about 20 m that are the observational unit; the transitional buffer is not used; and the economics explicitly set aside the choice of several inputs "for simplicity". The word "compensation" occurs once, for yield-monitor flow compensation. Bullock 2020 (abstract only) does not support the rule: its benchmark is the MRTN recommendation, not a grower status quo, and its object is the ex-ante value of the information a trial produces, not the realised return of the trial as applied. Puntel 2024 (full text) supports the two things the DIFM papers do not: partnering farmers were provided with financial compensation for the cost and risk of the trial N plan, and the farmer's business-as-usual management was compared with the tested technology on a whole-field basis in N, yield, partial profit and NUE; no formula, residual mechanics or payment terms are given. Gardner 2021 (abstract only) is the closest framework: run the randomised experiment, estimate the response for the Rx's zones, then test whether the Rx is economically optimal, on a 2018 nitrogen × seed-rate field; its benchmark and cost conventions sit in the paywalled body. A local DIFM platform report shows that DIFM's own number is an estimated optimal-uniform versus grower-chosen difference on the predicted profit surface, not a realised accounting.

**Support.** House convention. The per-unit counterfactual with the residual kept, the area-weighted ex-post accounting, the one-cost-with-every-input-at-status-quo rule and the 0.5 $/ac "cost nothing" band have no published counterpart. Bullock 2019 backs the baseline and the unit, Puntel 2024 the practice of compensating and of scoring against business-as-usual, Gardner 2021 the framework.

**What changed after the check.** The label "DIFM grower compensation" was a misnomer and was replaced in the PDF by "the ex-post cost of the trial to the grower, the quantity on-farm experimentation programmes compensate (DIFM pays a fixed amount per acre; Puntel 2024), computed here from the realised units". Bullock 2020 is no longer cited for it; Bullock 2019 is cited only for the grower-declared baseline. With two inputs the cost is quoted once, from the joint counterfactual, in place of one sentence per input.

### E9. A status quo outside the tested range; optima never leave it

**What the app does.** Optima are searched on a rate grid inside the trial range [lo, hi] only; an optimum at either end is a corner and is flagged in the app and the PDF. A status quo (or a recommended flat rate) outside the range is priced by `economics.bounded_curve`: inside the range the curve is itself; outside, the yield is extended linearly from the nearest edge with the curve's one-sided slope at that edge, never its curvature, and floored at 0; a plateau curve is extended the same way but never with a negative slope, and below the range with the slope of its rising segment. The input is charged at its own rate. `sq_position` measures how far the rate lies outside as a share of the range; beyond 25 % (`SQ_MAX_OUT`) the status quo is not priced at all, its columns are dropped and the PDF says so. The docstring records why the edge value is not simply held: that gave a status quo under the trial the yield of the lowest rate for less input, so it could never lose to that rate.

**What the literature says.** "Optima never leave the range" has published precedents. Scharf 2005 (full text) constrained the EONR never to exceed the highest N rate tested, 280 kg N/ha, reports EONR spanning 0 to 280 kg N/ha in five of eight fields, and replaced a fitted curvature the data did not support (positive quadratic coefficient → linear; negative linear coefficient → unresponsive; a fit that did not converge → linear; the three cases together were 40 of 611 cells). Mieno 2024 (manuscript source and full text) uses the same convention in its simulations: where the estimated EONR falls below or above the trial rates, the minimum or maximum trial rate is used as the corner solution, and the trial rates are set 20 kg/ha beyond the quantiles of the true plateau points so that the range covers the optimum; a modelling practice, not guidance. Bachmaier 2012 (full text) gives the rationale for not using the fitted curvature outside the tested rates: the optimum estimate depends strongly on the N-level design, the area on which the model is fitted; the extreme levels dominate the regression through leverage; and the fit should be trusted in a neighbourhood of the optimum. The same section asks for designs that include zero and very high N, so it does not argue against wide ranges. Rajsic 2008 (abstract only) supplies the premise, the need for a sufficient range of rates in N field trials; Miguez 2022 (abstract only) adds that precision and accuracy of the optimum demand more rates and replications. Cerrato 1990 and Bullock 1994 (abstracts only) are functional-form papers: in Cerrato 1990 the optima run from 128 to 379 kg N/ha across five models at one price ratio; neither says anything about rates outside the tested range, and neither supports E9.

**Support.** Partly supported for the bound on optima and for the premise. The one-sided linear extension, the floor at 0, the 25 %-of-range cut-off and charging the input at its own rate while extending the yield are house choices with no reference.

**What changed after the check.** Cerrato 1990 and Bullock 1994 are no longer cited for E9 (they belong to R1); Scharf 2005, Mieno 2024 and Bachmaier 2012 were added; the extension rule, the floor and the cut-off are named here as house choices.

### E10. The continuous-NDVI prescription as a diagnostic

**What the app does.** `economics.ndvi_prescription` evaluates the rate × NDVI model at 20 quantile bins of the field's pixels, each at the area-weighted mean NDVI of its own pixels (not the bin midpoint, because the end bins run to a slough or a yard), and chooses each bin's rate inside the trial range. Two flags follow. A bin whose mean NDVI lies outside the NDVI range of the analysis units is marked out of range, and its share of the field area (`share_out_of_range`) is printed and hatched in the figure: its optimum rests on the interaction extrapolated beyond the trial. A bin whose optimum sits at an end of the range is a corner (`share_corner`): with the interaction the curvature changes sign along the NDVI, so a convex or still-rising bin curve has no interior optimum and takes whichever end is cheaper. When more than 25 % of the field area is at a corner, or corners occur at both ends, the PDF says the NDVI-model gain is not a usable prescription and is reported as a diagnostic only. The zones, not the pixel model, carry the verdict.

**What the literature says.** Mieno 2024 (full text) supports the two halves that have a counterpart: the corner-solution convention quoted under E9, and distrust of fine-resolution prescriptions built from locally varying curvature. The coefficient on N² is estimated inaccurately, so the range of estimated EONRs is far wider than the true one; nearly half of the true economic values of GWR-based VRA are negative; the bias reaches about $40/ha; and the authors expect the bias for regression with interaction terms of field characteristics too. Colaço 2018 (abstract and introduction) reports that about 25 % of sensor-based N studies showed economic losses and that recommendations from empirical response curves cannot be confidently generalised beyond the conditions of the experiment. Colaço 2019 (abstract only): sensor calibrations pooled over 19 years of wheat reached an R² of about 0.25 and their benefit over a conventional rate was insignificant; univariate NDVI models have low accuracy. Piikki 2022 (abstract and highlights): the field-mean N rate comes from on-farm experiments and the vegetation index only adds to or subtracts from it, with a leave-one-trial-out error of 14 kg N/ha; a VI-driven rate is a modulation around an experimentally determined mean. Franzen 2016 (full text) does not support the rule: it catalogues four in-season sensor algorithms whose floors and caps are internal, and its core idea that yield potential and N response are independent argues against an r*(NDVI) prescription rather than describing when to demote one.

**Support.** House convention. The triggers (share of the field at a corner above 25 % or corners at both ends; bins outside the units' NDVI range), the 20 bins and the consequence "diagnostic only" have no published counterpart. The distrust of locally varying curvature and the diagnostic-only role of a vegetation index are supported.

**What changed after the check.** Franzen 2016 was dropped from E10; Colaço 2019 and Piikki 2022 were added. The rule text said "most of its bins"; the criterion stated above is the code's.

### E11. One season is ex post

**What the app does.** Every gain is labelled ex post, this field and this season (E2); the Limits say the optimum of one year is not next year's recommendation and that the trial should be repeated before zone rates are acted on. The cross-fit (E3) protects against overfitting within the season, not against the next season; the price grid (E7) shows how much the answer depends on prices. A lower rate advised without a yield response carries the caveat that under-application costs more than over-application when the true response is steeper than estimated (E5).

**What the literature says.** Rajsic 2008 (abstract only; supports) is the source of the distinction: on seven Ontario corn sites over several years with four functional forms there was no statistical or economic basis for selecting one model; ex post optima varied widely between seasons while the ex ante recommendation was constant; the recommendation usually exceeded the optimum, but the few large gaps were under-recommendations on less productive sites; the profit gap was under $10/ha on about a third of the trials; and over-application costs less than under-application, so applying above the average to capture good years is reasonable. Puntel 2016 (full text) shows how much the season optimum moves: EONR 123–268 kg N/ha in continuous corn and 42–241 in soybean–corn (CV 22 and 32 %), spring precipitation the only significant predictor (R² 0.27–0.45), and the determination is made after harvest, when yields are known. Mamo 2003 (abstract only) is the zone version: about half a Minnesota field responded to N, zone EONRs would have used 69 (1997) and 75 (1999) kg N/ha less and returned $8 and $23/ha more than a uniform 145 kg N/ha, and about 60 % of the field responded the same way in two years, so temporal variation must be weighed with spatial variation. Bullock 2020 (abstract only; partly) is a simulation on one low-heterogeneity Illinois field: fifteen years of OFPE did not make site-specific N profitable, while a few years improved the uniform rate against MRTN. Pannell 2006 and Pannell 2019 (abstracts; partly) give the flat-payoff reason the loss from an ex ante rate is usually small (E7). Lacoste 2022 (full text; partly) is the weakest: "plan, act, observe, reflect and repeat" and local replication are a participatory learning cycle, and the words ex ante, ex post and season do not occur in the paper; it is kept only for the on-farm-experimentation framing.

**Support.** Well supported. House choices: the number of seasons before zone rates may be acted on is unquantified in any source (Bullock 2020: a few versus more than fifteen, for one simulated field; Mamo 2003: 60 % of a field consistent in two of three years), and "loss usually small" is quantified for Ontario and Minnesota corn N and Western Australian lime, not for Prairie cereal seed or N rates.

**What changed after the check.** Puntel 2016 and Mamo 2003 were added; Lacoste 2022 was demoted to framing; the rule now says that the size of the ex ante loss is a corn and lime figure and carries the asymmetry of Rajsic 2008 (under-application is the costlier error) that E5 already uses.

## 9. Prices, units and Prairie context

### P1. Urea 46-0-0, nutrient basis and bushel weights

**What the app does.** `engine/prices.py` holds the conversions. Every rate inside the engine, the PDF and the prescription is lb/ac in the input's basis: lb of product (seed; urea as applied) or lb of nutrient for an input priced per lb N. A product's nutrient fraction converts between the two: urea 46-0-0 0.46, anhydrous ammonia 82-0-0 0.82, UAN 28-0-0 0.28, ESN 44-0-0 0.44, MAP 11-52-0 0.52 (P2O5); the price per rate unit is the product price divided by the fraction when the rates are nutrient ($/lb N = $/lb urea / 0.46). Grain prices are entered per bushel and converted from $/tonne with the crop's bushel weight (wheat and durum 60, barley 48, oats 34, canola 50, peas and lentils 60, flax 56, corn 56, soybeans 60, rye 56 lb; 2,204.62 lb per tonne). A design or map in kg/ha is converted on loading (× 0.892179); an as-applied map that logs lb of product under a price per lb N is converted with the fraction, and step 1 asks what the map logs. The join checks the applied rates against the design rates and warns when a basis is wrong (J3).

**What the literature says.** None of this is a research finding. 46-0-0 is the guaranteed analysis of urea under the Fertilizers Act and Regulations (pure urea is 46.65 % N, so × 0.46 undercounts N by about 1.4 %, the label guarantee); $/lb N = $/lb urea / 0.46 is algebra; the bushel weights are Canadian Grain Commission conversion factors with no DOI-bearing source. Khakbazan 2013 (full text) is the Prairie paper that documents the practice: the non-coated urea was commercial-grade fertiliser containing 46 % N, Table 2 lists "Urea (46-0-0)" at $0.584/kg of product (ESN, 44 % N, $0.704/kg), rates are in kg N/ha (25/50/75, 20/40/60 and 30/60/90 by site), crop prices in $/kg (wheat 0.220, canola 0.495, barley 0.190), and N rate was the main variable explaining net revenue in wheat, barley and canola. Fast 2024 (full text) prices CWRS at $436/Mg and urea at $1,100/Mg of product (Table 3) with rates in kg N/ha and NET = Y × P − C × NR, but never states 46 %, 0.46 or a per-bushel price, and its printed equation leaves the product-to-N cost conversion unstated, so it cannot be cited for the conversion. Malhi 2001 (abstract, introduction and section snippets; body paywalled) is an N-management review with nothing on units or prices and does not support P1. Cerrato 1990 (abstract only) is cross-cited for the point that the optimum uses a fertiliser-to-grain price ratio in consistent nutrient units. Both Prairie economic papers price grain per tonne or per kg, not per bushel.

**Support.** House convention anchored on standards. The 46-0-0 and product-pricing parts are documented Prairie practice (Khakbazan 2013); the bushel weights, the nutrient fractions of the other products and the kg/ha factor are standards cited as such.

**What changed after the check.** Khakbazan 2013 replaces Fast 2024 and Malhi 2001 as the citation for the 46-0-0 sentence; Fast 2024 is kept for the Prairie net-return frame only; Malhi 2001 is dropped from P1; the 46-0-0 and bushel-weight standards are named as standards without a DOI.

### P2. Prairie agronomy context

**What the app does.** P2 is interpretive, not computational. The app enforces no plausibility window; the context shapes how the report's text and the analyst read a result: a shallow seed-rate response in a cereal once the stand is adequate, an N response that plateaus, and in canola a response to both density and N. The defaults are malting barley about 300 seeds/m², a spring wheat and barley N plateau at 80–120 kg N/ha depending on moisture, and canola 100–150 seeds/m² at soil-test N.

**What the literature says.** Barley seed rate: O'Donovan 2012 (abstract only) tested 100–500 seeds/m² on AC Metcalfe over 24 site-years and found 300 seeds/m² usually optimal, with a risk of lower yield and plumpness above it. McKenzie 2005 (abstract only): 150–350 viable seeds/m² gave small yield gains, and maximum yield needed fertiliser plus available soil N above 31 kg N per Mg of yield, a yield-scaled rule, not a fertiliser band. O'Donovan 2011 (abstract only) tested 200 and 400 seeds/m² and N at 0–120 kg/ha with yield still rising at 120: no plateau, and no 300 seeds/m². Wheat seed rate: Lafond 1994 (abstract only) found yield rising with seeding rate in all crops and years on a thin Black soil (wheat 34–202, barley 27–161 kg/ha) and related to the reciprocal of seeding rate; Collier 2021 (abstract only) found 400 better than 200 seeds/m² in ultra-early systems; Beres 2016 (abstract and Core Ideas) is winter wheat, where a treated 200 seeds/m² stand yielded like 400 and 400 returned −$11/ha; McKenzie 2011 (abstract only) is irrigated, with seeding rate mattering less than date. Wheat N: Fast 2024 (full text): 60–240 kg N/ha, 120 kg N/ha optimal for CWRS wheat when coupled with enhanced-efficiency fertilisers; Read 1982 (abstract only): on fallow in south-western Saskatchewan rates above 15 kg N/ha gave little gain and on stubble the return fell to 3.1 kg grain per kg N between 45 and 60 kg N/ha, so the Brown soil zone plateaus under 60; Campbell 1977 (abstract only): one-site lysimeters at two moisture levels, diminishing returns irrigated but a logarithmic curve dryland, dryland plants lost 20 % of their N above 61.5 kg N/ha; Mangin 2022a (abstract only): NUE of 60 % at high N and 21–36 % of uptake after anthesis, dependent on late-season rain. Mangin 2022 (abstract only) has no seeding-rate factor and does not support P2. Canola: Harker 2012 (abstract only): 150 versus 75 seeds/m² gave +0.07–0.16 Mg/ha and +50 % N up to +0.25 Mg/ha across eight sites; Harker 2017a (abstract only): 100 to 150 seeds/m² did not raise yield at soil-test N, and novel inputs did nothing; Harker 2017 (abstract only): only small seed responds over 50–150 seeds/m²; Angadi 2003 (abstract only): yield flat from 20 to 80 plants/m² in a normal year and declining below 40 in a dry one; Yang 2014 (abstract only): the density effect is strongest below 60 plants/m²; Malhi 2007 (abstract only): N 0–150 kg N/ha economics by cultivar on three Saskatchewan soils. Grant 2012 could not be read by any route; of Malhi 2001 only the abstract, introduction and section snippets could be read (see P1), and none of them states an 80–120 kg N/ha plateau.

**Support.** Partly supported. The 80–120 kg N/ha band and the spring-wheat seeding window are syntheses, i.e. house choices: no paper states the band, barley is still rising at 120 kg N/ha (O'Donovan 2011) and the dry zone plateaus under 60 (Read 1982).

**What changed after the check.** Two factual errors were corrected: O'Donovan 2011 never tested 300 seeds/m² (the figure is O'Donovan 2012), and Mangin 2022 has no seeding-rate factor. Read 1982, Campbell 1977, Lafond 1994, Angadi 2003, Yang 2014, Malhi 2007, Harker 2017 and Harker 2017a were added; the band and the windows are presented as defaults with the soil-zone caveat; Malhi 2001 and Grant 2012 are cited only for what their titles support. The Prairie NDVI–yield timing of Mkhabela 2011 (abstract only; MODIS at census-region scale: best prediction window late June to late July in the sub-humid zone, early July to early August in the semi-arid and arid zones) is cited in the report's zone text (Z1), not here.
